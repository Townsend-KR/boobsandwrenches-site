# Stop Asking Screws to Be Joinery

*Shop Note · Wood Joinery · Structural Design*

Screws are excellent fasteners. They are also frequently handed a job that should belong to the shape of the joint. They are also frequently handed a job that should belong to the shape of the joint.

Put two boards together with a plain butt joint and every meaningful force has to cross that seam through a handful of screws. The screw shanks resist shear, the threads resist withdrawal, and the surrounding wood fibers carry concentrated pressure around each hole. That can work perfectly well for light-duty construction, but the screws are doing nearly all of the structural work.

Repeated loading, vibration, seasonal wood movement, or racking can gradually crush those fibers and enlarge the holes. Once that happens, the joint begins to move—and moving joints rarely improve with age.

What a Lap Joint Changes

A lap joint changes the load path.

Instead of merely placing one board against another, material is removed so the pieces physically overlap. In a typical half-lap, approximately half the thickness is removed from each member, allowing the finished pieces to sit flush.

That overlap creates broad wood-to-wood bearing surfaces and shoulders that resist sliding and rotation. Compression can travel directly through the wood, shear is distributed across a larger area, and the increased long-grain glue surface creates a much stronger bond than a small end-grain connection.

The screws no longer have to impersonate an entire engineering department. They can hold the joint closed, resist separation, and support the connection while the geometry and glue carry most of the everyday load.

Why It Matters in the Shop

A properly fitted lap joint can provide several advantages over screws alone:

* It distributes force across a surface instead of concentrating it around a few holes.
* It mechanically limits sliding, twisting, and racking.
* It provides more useful glue area.
* It registers the pieces during assembly and helps keep them square.
* It can remain stable even if a fastener loosens slightly over time.

The variation should match the job.

An end or corner half-lap works well in frames, gates, and rectangular assemblies that must resist racking. A T-lap connects the end of one member to the middle of another. A cross-lap allows two members to intersect while remaining in the same plane. A notched or housed variation can give a rail, brace, or shelf an actual shoulder to rest on so gravity is carried by wood rather than by fastener shanks.

That last distinction is important. A screw can keep a rail attached to a leg. A properly placed shoulder gives the rail somewhere to sit.

The Part Where We Do Not Get Reckless

A lap joint is not automatically stronger merely because it required more sawdust.

Removing too much material weakens the remaining section. An over-deep notch, loose fit, damaged grain, or badly placed fastener can create a new failure point. The joint should fit snugly without requiring enough hammering to split the piece, and its shoulders should be clean, square, and positioned for the expected load direction.

Structural screws can also be extremely strong when properly selected and installed. The point is not that screws are bad. The point is that a well-designed joint should avoid making them carry every force by themselves.

For building framing, decks, overhead loads, or anything whose failure could injure someone, use the required engineered connectors and follow applicable plans and codes. Shop furniture and project joinery are not permission to improvise life-safety structures.

A Useful Design Test

Before driving the screws, imagine they disappear.

Would one member still have a shoulder, notch, or bearing surface supporting it? Would the pieces still resist sliding in the expected load direction?

If the answer is yes, the joint’s geometry is helping carry the load.

If the answer is no, the screws are not reinforcing the structure. They are the structure.

Good joinery lets the wood do some of the work. The screws are simply there to make sure it keeps doing it..
